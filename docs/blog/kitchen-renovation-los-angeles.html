Kitchen Renovation in Los Angeles: A Designer's Guide

The kitchen decisions that matter most — and the ones that can wait — from a team that designs and manages LA kitchen renovations start to finish.

Published May 20, 2026•11 min read•JAC Interiors

Of all the rooms in a Los Angeles home, the kitchen is the one that carries the most weight — financially, functionally, and emotionally. Buyers judge it first. Families spend the most time in it. And when something is wrong with it — the layout, the storage, the light — it's wrong every single day. A kitchen renovation in Los Angeles is therefore one of the highest-stakes design decisions a homeowner can make, and one that benefits more than almost any other project from being done thoughtfully, with the right team, in the right sequence.

LA kitchens face a specific set of pressures that kitchens elsewhere don't. Open-plan living is nearly universal in remodelled homes here, which means the kitchen is visible from the living and dining areas at almost all times — it needs to look as considered as the rest of the space, not just function well on its own. Resale expectations in this market are high, and buyers in LA are discerning; a kitchen that was renovated without strong design discipline reads immediately. And the permit environment in Los Angeles adds complexity to any kitchen renovation that involves moving plumbing, upgrading electrical, or removing walls.

Here is what we've learned from designing and managing kitchen renovations across Los Angeles — the decisions that matter most, and the ones that can wait.

Design before demolition

The most expensive mistake in a kitchen renovation is not a materials choice or an appliance upgrade. It's changing your mind after construction has started. A revised countertop edge profile costs nothing on paper. The same change after the slabs have been fabricated costs the full re-fabrication fee, which can run $3,000 to $8,000 depending on material and scope. Moving a plumbing stack that was set in the wrong position because the sink location wasn't finalised before rough plumbing started can add $5,000 to $15,000 to a project and weeks to the timeline.

This is why we always begin a kitchen renovation with design — and specifically with a kitchen designer, not a contractor. A contractor's primary skill is execution; their sequencing is optimised for getting work done in a logical construction order. A designer's primary skill is problem-solving — understanding how the space needs to function, what it needs to look like, and how every decision connects to every other decision before a single wall comes down. The design phase is where you find the problems that would otherwise surface mid-construction, when they cost real money.

A properly conducted kitchen design process covers floor plan and layout options, the cabinetry configuration, countertop and backsplash selections, appliance specifications, lighting design, and the relationship of the kitchen to adjacent spaces — all resolved on paper before demolition begins. Clients who have been through a thorough design process find construction smoother, faster, and significantly less stressful than those who design as they go.

Layout: beyond the work triangle

The work triangle — the idea that the sink, range, and refrigerator should form an efficient triangle — was a useful framework for the closed kitchens of the mid-twentieth century. In the open-plan LA home, it's an incomplete way to think about kitchen layout. Today's kitchens are designed around zones: prep, cook, clean, storage, and social.

The prep zone is typically anchored at the primary countertop, ideally close to the refrigerator and with good proximity to the sink. The cook zone centres on the range or cooktop, with the ventilation directly above and adequate landing space on both sides. The clean zone — sink, dishwasher, and drying area — benefits from being kept away from the main traffic path so that someone loading the dishwasher isn't blocking the primary work flow. The storage zone encompasses both the pantry or tall cabinet run and the base cabinet storage near point of use. And the social zone — the seating at the island or counter where people congregate — needs to exist in a position where guests or family can be present without interrupting work flow.

In an open-plan kitchen, this zone thinking becomes even more important because the kitchen is visible and permeable from the living space. The social zone needs to face the room, not the wall. The clean zone — the less photogenic side of kitchen life — benefits from being positioned away from the main sightlines. The cook zone needs ventilation that doesn't compromise the visual connection between kitchen and living area.

The island versus peninsula question is one we work through carefully with every client. An island offers four-sided accessibility and is typically preferable in larger kitchens with strong traffic flow around it. A peninsula uses one wall for support, which can be advantageous in kitchens where floor space is tighter or where a defined separation between kitchen and dining is desirable. The island is the right choice for most open-plan LA kitchens with adequate square footage; the peninsula is worth considering seriously in smaller spaces or when a breakfast bar that reads as part of the dining zone is the goal.

Materials: countertops, cabinetry, backsplash, and flooring

Material selection in an LA kitchen renovation involves both aesthetic judgment and practical understanding of how materials perform in the context of real life. The choices that look right in a showroom and the choices that hold up over five years of daily use are not always the same.

Countertops. The three materials we work with most frequently are quartzite, quartz, and marble, each with a meaningfully different performance and maintenance profile. Quartzite — a natural stone that is often confused with quartz — offers exceptional hardness and durability with the veining character of a natural material. It requires sealing but holds up well to heat and daily use; it's our most frequently recommended choice for clients who want a natural stone look with strong performance. Quartz (engineered stone) offers near-zero maintenance, consistent patterning, and excellent durability, but lacks the depth and variation of natural stone and can be susceptible to UV yellowing near windows in sunny LA homes — worth knowing if the countertop will see direct sunlight. Marble is the most beautiful and the most demanding: it etches readily from acids (citrus, wine, coffee), stains without prompt attention, and requires regular maintenance. For clients committed to marble, we typically recommend its use on the perimeter countertops in lower-use areas, with a more durable material on the island where the daily work happens.

Cabinetry. The cabinetry is the largest single expenditure in most kitchen renovations and the material decision that most shapes the room's character. The painted versus stained decision is partly aesthetic and partly practical: painted cabinetry offers a clean, timeless look that works with virtually any countertop and is the dominant choice in LA kitchens right now; stained cabinetry has a warmth and material honesty that suits certain architectural styles, particularly mid-century and craftsman homes. Inset cabinetry — where the door sits flush inside the frame — is a higher-cost, more refined detail that signals quality immediately; overlay cabinetry (doors that cover the frame) is more common and perfectly appropriate in most contexts. Hardware direction — whether to use pulls, knobs, or no hardware at all — is a detail that seems small but affects the room's character significantly; integrated pulls or push-to-open mechanisms are the current preference in LA's more contemporary kitchens.

Backsplash. The backsplash is one place in a kitchen renovation where it's appropriate to take a considered risk. A well-chosen backsplash tile — whether a handmade ceramic, a stone slab that extends from the countertop, or a patterned encaustic — adds character in a way that's relatively low-cost to change in the future if tastes shift. We generally advise clients to invest in a backsplash that has some distinctiveness rather than defaulting to the safest option; the kitchen can absorb more personality at the backsplash than at the cabinetry or countertop.

Flooring. In an open-plan LA home, the kitchen floor is rarely just the kitchen floor — it typically continues into the dining area, the living room, or both. This makes flooring continuity a genuine design question, not just an aesthetic preference. A hard stop between kitchen tile and living room hardwood reads as a seam, visually dividing the space in a way that contradicts the open-plan intent. Wide-plank hardwood or large-format porcelain that runs continuously through the kitchen and into adjacent spaces is the stronger design move in most open-plan homes.

Appliances and ventilation

Appliance selection in a kitchen renovation involves decisions that feel like taste preferences but are actually structural. The ventilation system is the most consequential, and the most frequently underspecified.

In a closed kitchen, an underpowered range hood is an inconvenience. In an open-plan LA home, it's a significant problem — cooking odours, smoke, and airborne grease will move freely into the living and dining areas if the ventilation isn't adequate for the cooking that actually happens in the kitchen. Hood CFM (cubic feet per minute) is the relevant metric. A general guideline: 100 CFM per 10,000 BTU of gas burner output for a wall-mounted hood, with higher numbers required for island hoods, which are less efficient at capture. For a 48-inch professional-style range with 60,000 BTU output, an island hood should be running at least 1,000 CFM — a specification that requires a larger duct run, potentially roof penetration, and makeup air consideration. Getting the ventilation right is a design-phase decision, not a construction-phase one; the ductwork path needs to be planned before the ceiling is closed.

The pro-style versus high-quality residential appliance question is worth thinking through carefully. Pro-style appliances (48-inch ranges, built-in refrigeration, commercial-style hoods) are visually dominant and perform exceptionally; they're the right choice for serious cooks and for kitchens where the appliances are meant to be part of the room's character. High-quality residential appliances from brands like Miele, Bosch, or Wolf's residential line perform comparably for most home cooking and are often better suited to households that prioritise quiet operation, efficiency, and a more integrated aesthetic. Integrated refrigeration — where the refrigerator panels match the cabinetry — is worth considering seriously in any kitchen where the visual continuity of the cabinetry run matters; it's particularly impactful in smaller kitchens where a stainless refrigerator would read as a dominant visual interruption.

Budgeting a kitchen renovation in Los Angeles

Kitchen renovation budgets in Los Angeles span an enormous range, and understanding what you get at each level is important for setting realistic expectations before design begins.

At around $80,000, a kitchen renovation in LA can accomplish a meaningful transformation — new cabinetry, new countertops, updated appliances, new backsplash and fixtures — within the existing footprint and without significant structural or mechanical changes. This level typically involves semi-custom cabinetry (not fully custom), a strong but not top-tier countertop selection, and standard residential appliances. It's a genuine renovation, not a refresh, but it requires careful prioritisation to stay on budget.

At around $150,000, you have the full range of custom cabinetry options, premium countertop materials, higher-specification appliances, and the budget to address layout improvements that don't require major structural changes. This is the level where a kitchen renovation in LA becomes genuinely design-complete — where the material palette can be executed at the level the design deserves without significant compromise.

At $250,000 and above, you're working with fully custom cabinetry, top-tier natural stone countertops, professional-grade appliances, integrated refrigeration, and the structural and mechanical scope to move plumbing, expand the kitchen footprint, or address ventilation challenges that require significant ductwork. At this level, no material decision is forced by budget, which means the outcome is entirely a function of design quality.

Regardless of level, the allocation of budget within a kitchen renovation follows a consistent pattern. The cabinetry is where the money matters most — quality custom or semi-custom cabinetry is the decision that most determines how the kitchen looks and functions for the next twenty years. The countertop is the second most important investment. Ventilation is frequently under-budgeted and worth prioritising. Hardware and faucets, by contrast, are where modest savings are most appropriate; a well-chosen $200 pull reads as well as a $400 one, and a faucet can be upgraded later with minimal disruption.

Permits and timelines

A kitchen renovation in Los Angeles requires a permit any time the scope includes moving plumbing, upgrading or modifying the electrical panel or branch circuits, removing a load-bearing wall, or adding or relocating mechanical systems including ductwork. Many kitchen renovations — particularly those that involve an expanded footprint, a relocated island, or a ventilation upgrade that requires new roof penetration — will trigger permits. A renovation that involves purely cosmetic changes (new cabinetry in the same configuration, new countertops, new appliances in the same locations) typically does not, though it's always worth confirming with the relevant authority.

When permits are required, the timeline impact is significant. A standard over-the-counter kitchen permit in Los Angeles — straightforward plumbing and electrical scope, no structural changes — can be approved in days to a few weeks. A permit that requires plan check, or that involves structural work triggering engineering review, can run six weeks to several months before approval. During that period, construction cannot proceed on the permitted scope.

Working with a designer who understands the LA permit process changes this calculation meaningfully. The permit set — the drawings submitted for approval — needs to be accurate, complete, and in the format LADBS requires. An experienced design team will know what triggers a permit, how to prepare a set that moves through plan check efficiently, and how to sequence the project so that permit-dependent work doesn't hold up the entire job. Clients who hire a contractor and then discover mid-project that permits are required — and that the drawings needed for those permits don't exist — face both delay and additional cost to produce them retroactively.

Where to start

The best kitchen renovations we've worked on have one thing in common: they started with a clear picture of what the kitchen needed to do, and a design process that resolved every decision before construction began. The worst ones started with a demolition date and a rough idea of what the owners wanted, then made every material and layout decision under the pressure of an active job site.

If you're considering a kitchen renovation in Los Angeles, the right starting point is a conversation about what's not working in your current kitchen — the layout problems, the storage deficiencies, the ventilation issues, the aesthetic that's never felt right — and what you're trying to accomplish. That conversation shapes the design brief, and the design brief shapes everything that follows.
