How to design a luxury bathroom

A planning framework for the decisions that actually determine how a bathroom feels and functions — before a single material is chosen.

Published Aug 18, 20268 min readJAC Interiors

Most people start designing a bathroom by looking at tile. It's the fun part — scrolling through slabs, picking a favorite stone, imagining a finished room. But the bathrooms that end up feeling genuinely luxurious were never decided by material first. They were planned, in a specific order, long before anyone chose a finish.

A luxury bathroom isn't defined by how much was spent on it. It's defined by how few things feel like compromises. That comes from a sequence of decisions made deliberately, rather than in whatever order happens to come up during construction. Here's the order we actually use.

Start with how you use the room, not how it looks

Before any layout is drawn, we ask clients a set of unglamorous questions: Do two people get ready at the same time? Is this a bath person or a shower person? Does anyone need a seated shower now or in the foreseeable future? Is this bathroom also a retreat — a place to linger — or purely functional? The answers change the design more than any material decision that follows. A couple with staggered schedules needs a different vanity configuration than a couple who gets ready together. A household with no bath users at all shouldn't default to a freestanding tub just because it photographs well.

This is the step that gets skipped most often, and it's the one that determines whether the finished room actually works for daily life or just works for a photograph.

Get the layout right before anything else

Once we know how the room needs to function, layout comes next — and layout is where the real design work happens. The relationship between the shower, the tub (if there is one), the vanity, and the toilet has to resolve without dead space or awkward sightlines. In primary bathrooms with adequate square footage, separating the toilet into its own compartment is almost always worth doing. It lets the main room read as a single continuous space rather than a room with a toilet in the corner.

This is also the moment to decide whether to move plumbing. Keeping fixtures where they are controls cost, but it can lock you into a compromised layout. Moving a shower to catch morning light, or repositioning a vanity so it doesn't face the toilet, is often worth the added expense — but it needs to be a conscious decision made at this stage, not a workaround discovered mid-construction.

Bathrooms

Choose a material hierarchy, not a material list

The bathrooms that feel expensive rarely use the most materials — they use the fewest, chosen deliberately, in a clear order of importance. We think of it as a hierarchy: one primary surface (usually the stone or tile that covers the floor and shower), one secondary material (the vanity surface or an accent wall), and restrained metal and glass choices that support rather than compete.

The mistake we see most often is a room with four or five materials all fighting for attention — a patterned floor tile, a different accent wall tile, a third color on the vanity, brass fixtures, and black hardware. Individually, each choice might be good. Together, they read as indecisive. A luxury bathroom typically has one material doing the visual work and everything else quietly supporting it.

Plan lighting as a system, not an afterthought

Lighting is usually the last thing clients think about and the thing with the most outsized effect on how the finished room actually feels. A bathroom needs at least three layers: task lighting at face height on either side of the mirror (not overhead, which casts unflattering shadows), a dimmable ambient source for the rest of the room, and accent lighting that highlights the material choices you just spent so much time selecting. Planning this before construction — not after the drywall is up — is what makes recessed niches, cove lighting, and backlit mirrors possible at all.

Bathrooms

Decide where the room's "moment" is

Every well-designed bathroom has one focal point that does the heavy lifting — a book-matched stone wall behind the tub, a dramatic mirror, a fluted vanity, a piece of art in an unexpected spot. Deciding early where that moment lives keeps the rest of the room from competing with it. Once that decision is made, everything else — tile grout color, hardware finish, towel bar placement — gets easier, because it's being chosen in support of a clear idea rather than in isolation.
