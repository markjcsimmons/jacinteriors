Maximalist Interior Design

More is more — but only when every piece has earned its place. What separates curated abundance from clutter.

Published Aug 1, 2026•8 min read•JAC Interiors

Maximalism is the aesthetic most likely to be misread as its own failure. Done badly, it looks like a room nobody edited — pattern fighting pattern, color without logic, objects accumulated rather than chosen. Done well, it's one of the most difficult and most rewarding directions in residential design: a space dense with color, pattern, and personal history that still reads as considered, room by room, from the first glance.

The difference between the two has nothing to do with quantity. A maximalist room and a cluttered room can contain roughly the same number of objects. What separates them is intention — whether every piece is there because it belongs, or because nothing was ever removed. At JAC Interiors, we've taken on more maximalist projects in the past two years than in the decade before it, largely as a direct reaction to the all-neutral, all-restrained interiors that dominated the 2010s. Here's how we think about doing it properly.

Maximalism is an editing discipline, not an absence of one

The single biggest misconception about maximalism is that it requires no restraint. In practice, the opposite is true — a maximalist room requires more editorial judgment than a minimalist one, not less, because there are more decisions being made and more ways for those decisions to conflict. Every pattern has to be scaled against every other pattern in the room. Every color has to sit somewhere considered on the palette. Every object needs a reason beyond "we liked it and had space."

The rooms that fail at maximalism almost always fail for the same reason: they stopped editing. A collection became a pile. A palette became a free-for-all. The fix isn't to remove color and pattern — it's to bring back the same rigor a minimalist project would apply, just in service of abundance rather than restraint.

Building a palette that can hold that much color

A maximalist palette needs an anchor, even if the anchor isn't obvious at first glance. That usually means picking two or three dominant colors that appear repeatedly across the room — on upholstery, on walls, in art, in textiles — so that the eye has something to organize around even as the volume of pattern and object increases. Without that anchor, a room full of color reads as noise instead of richness.

Pattern follows a similar logic: mix scale deliberately. A large-scale floral or a bold stripe needs to be balanced against smaller, quieter patterns rather than paired with something equally loud. The most successful maximalist rooms we've built usually have one dominant pattern doing the visual heavy lifting — on a wallpaper, a rug, or major upholstery — with everything else in the room playing a supporting role in scale and intensity.

Collected objects, not staged ones

What makes maximalism feel genuine rather than performed is provenance — the sense that the objects in the room have a history, that they were found rather than ordered from a single catalogue in one sitting. Art collected over years. A side table found at an estate sale. Ceramics picked up while traveling. Family pieces reupholstered rather than replaced. These objects carry a specificity that no amount of styling can manufacture, and they're what keeps a maximalist room from feeling like a showroom.

This is where working with someone who genuinely sources — rather than simply specifies from a line sheet — matters most. We spend real time in antique shops, estate sales, and small dealers building rooms that have that quality of accumulation, because it's nearly impossible to fake convincingly and immediately obvious when it's missing.

Furniture: mixing eras with a reason

Maximalist rooms tend to mix furniture from different periods and traditions far more freely than a transitional or contemporary space would. An antique chest can sit beside a contemporary sofa; a Regency mirror can hang above a mid-century console. What makes the mix cohere is that every piece is chosen for a specific reason — scale, color, material, the way it plays against its neighbors — rather than arriving as a themed set.

The scale discipline matters here more than it does in more restrained interiors, precisely because there's more happening in the room. A large-scale piece needs breathing room even in a dense space; a small, delicate piece needs to be positioned where it won't disappear against everything competing for attention around it.

Where maximalism goes wrong

The most common failure point is treating maximalism as permission to stop making decisions — buying whatever appeals in the moment and trusting that "more is the aesthetic" will paper over the lack of a plan. It won't. A second common failure is applying the same intensity uniformly throughout a home, with no quieter rooms to let the eye reset. The most livable maximalist homes have a rhythm — a dense, richly layered living room or dining room, balanced by a bedroom or study that pulls back and lets the palette breathe.

Why it's resonating now

Maximalism's return is, in part, a direct response to a decade of restraint. Clients who lived in beautifully edited, neutral homes for years are increasingly asking for rooms with more personality, more color, more evidence of a life being lived in them. It's also a natural fit for South Florida, where Mediterranean Revival and Art Deco architecture already carry a level of ornament and color that a purely minimalist interior tends to fight rather than complement.

Done with real discipline, maximalism produces some of the most memorable interiors we build — rooms that reward a second and third look, that feel unmistakably specific to the people who live in them.
