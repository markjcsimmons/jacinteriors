Bathroom remodel cost in Los Angeles

What you'll actually spend on a bathroom remodel — and where the walk-in shower budget really goes.

Published Sep 5, 20268 min readJAC Interiors

After layout, cost is the question we get asked most on every bathroom project — and it's the one people are most likely to have the wrong number for going in. National cost guides and TV renovation shows set expectations that don't match what a bathroom actually costs to build well in Los Angeles or coastal South Florida. Here's a clearer picture of where the money goes and why.

We've run bathroom projects ranging from a single powder room refresh to a full primary suite rebuild, across the Westside, the Hills, the Valley, and South Florida. The ranges below reflect what we actually see, not a national average.

Why LA bathroom costs run higher than national averages

Three factors consistently push Los Angeles numbers above what national guides quote:

Labor. Licensed plumbers, electricians, and tile setters in LA charge 30–50% more than the national median, and bathroom work is unusually labor-intensive relative to material cost — waterproofing, tile setting, and fixture rough-in all take skilled hands and real time.

Waterproofing and permitting. Bathrooms involve plumbing, and often electrical, work that requires permits and inspection. Proper waterproofing — a correctly installed shower pan, membrane, and slope — is not optional and not fast. Cutting corners here doesn't save money; it creates a leak that costs far more to fix later.

Expectations. The homes most JAC clients are remodeling — Westside craftsmans, Hollywood Hills contemporaries, South Florida coastal properties — call for finishes proportional to the home. A primary bathroom in a $3M property needs different stone and fixtures than a national-average bathroom, and that shows up directly in the budget.

Price ranges by scope

With those factors in mind, here's how we categorize bathroom remodel budgets in LA:

$15,000–$30,000 — Powder room or selective update. This range covers a full powder room redesign (vanity, lighting, wallpaper or a feature material, flooring, fixtures) or a secondary bathroom refresh that keeps the existing layout and plumbing locations. Materials are mid-range tile or quartz, fixtures are quality but not top-tier, and no structural or plumbing relocation is involved.

$30,000–$70,000 — Full secondary or modest primary bath remodel. This is the most common range for a complete bathroom gut in a guest or secondary bath, or a straightforward primary bathroom where plumbing stays roughly in place. It typically includes a new walk-in shower with a glass panel, stone or high-end tile on the floor and shower walls, a custom or semi-custom vanity, and a full lighting redesign.

$70,000–$150,000+ — Luxury primary suite. At this level, the primary bathroom is often being reconfigured — plumbing moved, the toilet separated into its own compartment, a larger walk-in shower with multiple shower heads or steam capability, book-matched stone slabs, custom furniture-style vanities, and a fully layered lighting plan. In larger Westside, Hills, or South Florida coastal properties, this is the range where the bathroom matches the caliber of the rest of the home.

What drives the numbers

Within any budget range, a handful of decisions move the total more than people expect:

Stone and tile. Material is typically 25–35% of a bathroom budget, but the range within that is enormous. Mid-range porcelain tile might run $8–$15 per square foot installed. Book-matched marble or premium quartzite slabs can reach $150–$300 per square foot for material alone, and a shower surround uses a lot of square footage fast.

The walk-in shower system. This is usually the single largest line item after stone. A glass enclosure alone can run $2,500–$6,000 depending on size and hardware finish. Add a rain shower head, a separate hand shower, body sprays, or a steam system, and the plumbing and fixture package for one shower can reach $10,000–$25,000 before the tile is even installed.

Custom vanity and millwork. A stock or semi-custom vanity might run $2,000–$5,000. A fully custom, furniture-style double vanity with integrated lighting and specific stone can run $10,000–$25,000 or more, depending on size and material.

Plumbing relocation. Moving a toilet, shower, or vanity to a new location — rather than keeping fixtures in place — typically adds $3,000–$15,000, depending on what's involved and whether it's a slab or raised foundation.

Waterproofing and structural work. Proper waterproofing for a curbless, barrier-free shower, plus any subfloor leveling or structural adjustment, is often underestimated. Budget 10–15% of the project for this category even when nothing looks obviously wrong going in.

Hidden costs that catch people off guard

What's behind the walls. Bathrooms in older homes frequently reveal outdated plumbing, deteriorated subfloor, or prior water damage once demolition starts. This is the single most common source of mid-project budget increases in bathroom remodels specifically — more so than kitchens, where surprises are less frequent.

Temporary access. If this is the only full bathroom, or the only primary bathroom, arranging alternate access during the remodel is a real logistical and sometimes financial consideration, especially on projects running 6–10 weeks.

Lead times. Custom glass enclosures, specific stone slabs, and certain plumbing fixtures — especially from European brands — can have 6–12 week lead times. Ordering early, before demolition, keeps these from becoming the reason a finished project sits idle waiting on one part.

How to approach budgeting

Start by defining what actually matters to you: is it the shower experience, the stone, the vanity, or simply a layout that finally works? Build the budget around those non-negotiables first, then fit the remaining decisions into what's left. A contingency of 15–20% is standard for bathroom remodels specifically, given how often demolition reveals something that wasn't visible beforehand.
