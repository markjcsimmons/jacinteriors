Paint Color Trends 2026

Sage green, warm whites, moody grays, and teal — the palette shaping interiors this year, and how to use each one without dating a room.

Published Aug 1, 2026•8 min read•JAC Interiors

Paint is the cheapest, fastest, and most consequential decision in almost any interior project — a few gallons can change how a room reads entirely, for a fraction of the cost of anything else on a renovation list. It's also where trend cycles move fastest, which makes it worth being deliberate about which "2026" colors are genuinely shifting how people paint their homes and which are a passing moment on a design blog.

Here's what we're actually specifying on client projects this year, and — more importantly — how to use each of these colors so the room still looks considered in five years, not dated in two.

Sage green: the neutral that isn't white

Sage has quietly become the default "safe" color for clients who want more warmth and personality than white but aren't ready to commit to a bold color. It reads as calm and grounded rather than trend-driven, which is exactly why it's lasted longer than most green moments in paint history. We're using it most often on kitchen cabinetry, powder rooms, and as a wall color in spaces that get good natural light — sage can go flat and a little muddy in a north-facing room with limited daylight.

Shades worth knowing: Sherwin-Williams Evergreen Fog for something soft and slightly gray-toned, Farrow & Ball Yeabridge Green for a deeper, more saturated version, and Benjamin Moore Saybrook Sage if you want something closer to true sage with a bit more yellow in it. The deeper you go, the more it reads as a color statement rather than a neutral — decide which effect you actually want before choosing a depth.

Warm white, still winning over stark white

Stark, cool white has been losing ground for years, and 2026 hasn't reversed that. What's changed is which warm whites are getting specified — clients and designers alike have gotten more precise about avoiding whites that tip yellow or cream in a way that reads dated, favoring whites with just enough warmth to feel inviting without looking like an off-white from a decade ago.

Benjamin Moore Swiss Coffee remains a dependable, barely-there warm white that works in almost any light. Farrow & Ball Shaded White adds a touch more depth and works especially well against natural wood and stone. The test we use with clients: paint a large sample board and view it at both 9am and 6pm in the actual room — a warm white that looks right in the morning can shift noticeably by evening, and it needs to hold up in both.

Frances

Gray, but warmer and deeper than the 2010s version

Gray never left, but the gray of 2026 is a different animal from the cool, flat "greige" that dominated a decade ago. What's being specified now is either much warmer — closer to a true greige with brown undertones — or much deeper, moving into charcoal and near-black tones used deliberately on millwork, doors, and accent walls rather than entire rooms.

For a warm greige, Farrow & Ball Purbeck Stone holds up well against both cool and warm materials in the same room. For depth, Benjamin Moore Chelsea Gray works as a sophisticated mid-tone, and Farrow & Ball Downpipe is the go-to when a client wants a genuinely dark, almost-black charcoal on a door, a study, or a powder room without committing to true black.

Teal as the grounding accent

Teal has moved from statement color to something closer to a workhorse accent — used on a single wall, a kitchen island, built-in cabinetry, or a powder room, rather than an entire open living space. It reads as sophisticated rather than playful when it's deep and slightly muted, and it pairs unusually well with brass hardware and natural wood tones, which is likely why it's held on longer than most saturated accent colors typically do.

Benjamin Moore Aegean Teal is deep enough to anchor a room without going navy, and Farrow & Ball Vardo leans a touch greener, which suits spaces already built around sage or warm wood tones. As with any saturated color, we test it on the actual wall, in the actual light, before committing — teal shifts more dramatically between daylight and lamplight than most colors on this list.

Highland

Choosing across a whole house, not room by room

The mistake we see most often isn't a bad individual color choice — it's a house that reads as a series of unrelated trend decisions, room to room, with no connective logic. Before specifying any of the colors above, we build a whole-house palette first: a dominant neutral that carries through most rooms, one or two accent colors that repeat with intention, and a clear plan for where deeper or bolder colors are allowed to appear. A sage kitchen and a teal powder room can absolutely coexist in the same home — they just need a shared warm-white or greige backbone tying them together, rather than each room chasing its own trend independently.

Testing before committing

Every color on this list looks different in a paint app, on a chip, and on an actual wall — and the gap between those three is larger than most people expect, especially with saturated colors like teal and deep charcoal. Paint a sample at least two feet square directly on the wall, in more than one location in the room, and live with it for a few days across different times of day before ordering gallons. It's a small amount of extra patience that prevents the single most common and most expensive paint mistake: repainting a room that looked right on the sample chip and wrong at scale.
